## Why it exists

The project started with a colleague who had just taken the job. Most of what he told were stories about drinking too much, and he had studied mathematics. The useful connection was between those two facts. A recipe is a set of quantities. Change one quantity and the finished drink should change in a way you can calculate: color, alcohol, sweetness, and whether the liquids blend or sit in layers. That calculation is the app.

## Who it is for

It is for someone mixing at home. The cabinet screen is for a partial shelf, the bottles already in the house. The recipe screen is for a person who wants to change a drink and see the glass before pouring it. Ask Bob is for the part of drinking alone that is a conversation: you say how the day went, and you still leave with one real drink from the menu.

## What sets it apart

Cocktail apps are catalogs. This one keeps the catalog, then lets the recipe be edited, and draws the edit. Volume, color, alcohol, and sweetness are recomputed from the ingredients in the glass. A layered drink shows the change as bands, ordered by density, so a shot makes the difference obvious. A substitute is another ingredient in the same family, ranked by how close it is in alcohol, sweetness, acidity, and color. Bob can miss the way a person misses. The glass follows the numbers.

## How it is put together

The phone is React Native on Expo, in five tabs: Library, My Cabinet, Search, Ask Bob, and Saved. The service behind it is FastAPI. Recipes began as TheCocktailDB entries and were normalized into milliliters, a method, a glass, and a physics baseline for each ingredient: alcohol by volume, Brix, acidity, color, and how opaque it is. Photographs on the cards still come from TheCocktailDB. The glass is calculated on the phone, so moving a measure does not wait on the network. Bob and the voice transcription run on the service. The app was built in Cursor, with Gemini alongside the work.

## Library

Each family is a horizontal row: highballs, sours, and the rest. A card shows the drink and its glass, and opens the recipe. The heart writes that recipe's id into storage on the phone. Saved reads the same list.

## Cabinet

The bottles you mark stay on the phone. Matching is a set difference against every recipe. Nothing missing means a perfect match. One or two missing means almost there, and those names are shown on the card. The closer drinks are sorted first.

## Search

The names are already on the phone, and each letter filters them. A name that contains what you typed is shown as an exact hit. If nothing contains it, the search counts how many edits would turn the query into a recipe name, keeps the ones within three edits, and shows the five closest. A misspelling still lands on a drink, and the keystrokes stay on the device.

Bob is instructed to answer the mood first, then name exactly one drink from the menu he was given. He does not invent a cocktail. The reply comes back as two fields: what he says, and the drink's name. The service checks that name against the catalog, an exact match first and then a partial one, and attaches the real recipe so the card can open it. You can type, or hold the button and talk. The recording is transcribed with Whisper and sent on as the message. The thread stays above the keyboard while you write.

The model is Llama 3.1, called through Groq, with a short reply and a fixed shape so the phone can trust the fields. A recommendation that is not on the menu is dropped, and Bob is left as conversation.

## Saved

The heart stores a recipe id. Saved is that list, drawn as the same cards, so a drink you changed, or one Bob named, can be opened again without searching for it.

## The recipe

The screen opens on the photograph. The other view draws the glass. Seven glasses are modeled, each with a capacity, and the liquid is clipped to that shape. Pass the capacity and the fill stops at the rim. Changing the glass, the measure, or an ingredient switches into the drawing, because the photograph is no longer the drink you are making.

Alcohol and sweetness are volume-weighted averages of whatever is in the glass. Color is mixed by how much light each ingredient absorbs, then turned back into a color, so a dark bottle darkens the result instead of being averaged away. In the layered state, each ingredient is given a density from its sweetness and its alcohol, and the denser liquid is drawn underneath. Mixed state uses one blended color. Under the drawing, alcohol, sweetness, and volume are shown against the original recipe, with the direction of the change. A swap also writes a short note: stronger, milder, sweeter, drier, or close to where it started. Substitutes are other ingredients in the same category, ranked by the distance between those physical values.

## How it was made

The phone, the physics drawing, and the service were built in Cursor, with Gemini used alongside the implementation. The video above is the piece you can hold.
