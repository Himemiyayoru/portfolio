## Who she is

Hime is a companion who lives on the same computer as the person she talks to. The project began with Neuro-sama: a voice, a longer mind behind it, and a face that is actually driven. She is not a public stream. She is written as my younger sister, a little tsundere, sharp when she wants to be, and easy to like.

The Live2D model is used with permission from a friend, and only so the system can be shown working. The contributor is 椰蓉球.

The first version was written with Gemini: language, memory, and control of the virtual face. The work then moved to Cursor, where those three parts were tightened and the vision module was written.

## One queue

The microphone, the keyboard, and the screen used to arrive as separate calls. They are now one priority queue. A spoken line, a typed line, and a note from the screen are the same kind of event. The person's voice sits at the top. When they start talking, a waiting comment from the screen is cleared, and her own playback can be stopped. Echo from the speakers is ignored while she is the one speaking, so her voice is not transcribed back in as a new question. A slot is already reserved for audience chat. Nothing publishes into it yet.

Hearing is Whisper large v3. Chinese, English, and Japanese are detected from the utterance, then checked against the letters. Noise, and a lone hallucinated phrase, are dropped. A real sentence that happens to contain those words is kept. One utterance may run up to a minute. The end of a turn is a short silence, about a third of a second, not a hard cut in the middle of a long thought. A typed line joins the same queue, and its language is guessed from the text.

The reply is one streamed model. An earlier design spoke a short lead-in from a fast model and then handed the rest to a slower one. The two clocks drifted whenever either model changed, and the gap was audible. One model removes the handoff. Clauses are cut on punctuation as they arrive and sent to speech immediately, so the voice starts while the rest of the line is still being written. A fragment that is only punctuation is absorbed into the next clause, instead of becoming a near-silent request.

## A trained voice

The speaking voice is a local GPT-SoVITS timbre, trained for this character rather than taken from a stock narrator. At synthesis time each request carries a reference recording of that voice and the transcript of the clip, which is what holds the timbre in place. Chinese, English, and Japanese can each keep their own reference. The name Hime is always split out and voiced on the Japanese path, because the name does not change pronunciation with the sentence around it.

A mixed sentence is split by script. Every segment is requested at once, edge silence is trimmed, and the pieces are crossfaded into one clip, so a change of language does not click. Words inside parentheses are stage directions. They are stripped before synthesis, including a bracket that is still open at the end of one streamed clause. The next clause inherits that state, so an action is not suddenly read aloud. Playback can be interrupted the moment the person starts talking.

## Memory

After a turn, a second, colder pass reads the exchange and keeps only a new event: a plan, a preference, an appointment. Small talk, and a restatement of who she already is, comes back as nothing and is not stored. What is kept is stamped with the real date and time, cut on sentence boundaries, and embedded with M3E into a ChromaDB collection. A later reply can retrieve the entries that match.

What she sees is a second collection, embedded with CLIP, so a short visual note can be found again without being mixed into the chat bank. Near-duplicate descriptions are dropped. On a still page the filter is strict. In a game or a video it is looser, because two similar sentences can be a fight that has actually moved.

## The face

On connection, VTube Studio returns every hotkey the loaded model publishes: expressions, gestures, one-shot animations, and the toggles for hair, clothes, and accessories. Hime caches that full list, with the type the studio reports, and checks every configured name against it. A missing name is printed at startup instead of failing in silence later. Any of those hotkeys can be opened or closed through one ledger. A toggle that is already on is not triggered again, because a second trigger would turn it off. A one-shot animation is left alone. When the turn ends, every toggle she opened is closed, whether it came from an emotion tag or from a word in the line.

Five emotion tags, which the reply must emit, map onto expressions on this model: a heart-eyed look, anger, crying, a shocked sweat, and a neutral that clears the slot. Separately, words in her own dialogue fire a wider set of preset reactions: blushing, a blank stare, leaning in, holding a cat, a drink, a small crown, eyes lighting up. The gesture starts when the audio of that clause starts, not when the text is generated, so it lands on the word. Several words in one clause can fire in order.

While she is quiet the head still moves. Each angle walks toward a new random target every fraction of a second, with a light breath and an occasional small bounce, instead of looping one sine wave. The size of that motion follows the current mood and how loud she is. Happy and shock open the movement up. Sadness quiets it.

The mouth is driven from her own audio, not from the microphone, so headphones do not close it. Loudness sets how far it opens. The shape is a vowel timeline built from the exact text sent to speech: pinyin for Chinese, a kana row for Japanese, a letter scan for Latin. The name Hime falls out of that scan as i, then e. The vowels are spread across the length of the clip and advanced by the same clock as the loudness, so a shape is active only while its audio is playing. A silence gate on the model, the switch between the default mouth and the vowel blend shapes, is the inverse of that loudness. Silence keeps the mouth closed and the gate shut.

## The screen

A local loop grabs the display about five times a second. It does not send every frame to a model. A small grayscale difference against the previous tick decides whether the picture is actually changing, and that decision is remembered for about three seconds, so one flicker does not have to land on the exact sample.

The focused window picks the scene. The keyword lists live in configuration: a game, a video player or a video site, a code editor, a browser, or idle. Video is tested before the generic browser list, so a tab that is playing a video is not classified as a page of text.

Each scene has its own rate and its own question. Idle backs off to about fifteen seconds. A still page is about six. A game, or a video that is actually moving, tightens to about two and a half seconds, and falls back if the video is paused. An editor does not use screenshots at all. UI Automation reads the text in the editor about every two seconds, which is faster and does not spend a vision call on a picture of code.

A game or a moving video is not described from one frozen frame. Three recent frames, about four fifths of a second apart, go out together, oldest first, and the prompt asks what is changing across them. A game prompt asks for the title, the moment, and anything urgent. A page prompt asks for the topic and the kind of content. A paused video gets a single frame. The notes are written as a short diary on the machine and embedded for later retrieval. They are not published on this site.

A separate pixel path is reserved for a calibrated game: health, an ultimate, a kill feed. Those regions are not filled in, because a wrong coordinate is a detector that never fires. Until a session is measured, the general vision model is the path that works for any title. The next training is the one that lets her understand the rules, and then play the character, rather than only describe the screen.
